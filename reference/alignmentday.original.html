00 Before we startRead out loud ▾

The rules matter more than the questions.

Why this matters

Our first conversation didn't fail on content. It failed because it was rushed and because neither of us felt fully safe saying the true version. If we get this part right, everything after it works. If we skip it, we'll just have the same rushed conversation again with better questions.

How the day runs

Do this
  • Two notebooks and two pens. Several parts ask us to write separately first — that only works on paper.
  • Phones away except this page. Both of us, not one of us.
  • Break properly when we need it. Nothing here has to be finished today, and we can stop halfway and continue another day.
  • Read the green box at the start of each part out loud before beginning it.

The five rules

  • Both of us answer everything.

    If one person is only asking, this has stopped being a conversation and become an interview.

  • Curiosity before verdict.

    The first response to anything we hear is a question, not a reaction. "Tell me more" before "I don't like that."

  • Either of us can pause, no reason needed.

    The Pause button at the bottom of the screen. Pausing is not avoiding.

  • Nothing is settled by exhaustion.

    If one of us gives in just to end it, we haven't agreed on anything — we've postponed it with extra resentment attached.

  • Honesty gets thanked, not punished.

    Big enough that it gets its own box, below.
Note — say this out loud, together

The amnesty clause. Everything either of us says today — about our past, our fears, the things we've never said — is being given voluntarily. It's a gift, not a confession. So before Part 01 we agree:

  • What gets told today does not get thrown back later, in an argument or anywhere else.
  • Being honest about something never makes it a bigger problem than hiding it would have been. If honesty gets punished here, we're just teaching each other to stop being honest.
  • Asking to understand is allowed. Prosecuting is not. Either of us can say "that's a verdict, not a question" and the other resets without arguing about whether it was fair.
  • Neither of us has to defend who we were before we met.
  • Nobody should walk away from today wishing they'd said less. If that happens, the day failed — whatever we concluded.

One each, before we begin

  • Now

    Name one thing you do that makes the other person stop talking.

    Your own, not theirs. Going quiet, getting logical, getting hurt fast, explaining instead of listening, making a joke. Name it now so we can both catch it later without it being an attack. Say it like this“When I feel ___, I tend to ___, and I know that makes you stop.”
  • Now

    What are you most hoping comes out of today, and what are you most afraid of?

01 Our six monthsLooking back · honestly ▾

Before we talk about the life we want, we look at the one we've already been living.

Why this matters

Six months is real data, and most of what we need to know is already inside it. Everything we say about the future is a guess; this is the only part of the day where we're working with evidence. If we're honest here, the rest of the day gets much easier.

The timeline

Do this

Separately, without talking, each of us writes down three high points and two hardest moments from our six months. Just the moments — no explanations yet. Then compare.

  • Six months

    Do our lists overlap?

    A moment one of us marked as a high and the other doesn't even remember is worth more than an hour of theorising.
  • Six months

    What do the high points have in common? What do the hard ones have in common?

    Look for the ingredient, not the event. Time alone together? Feeling chosen? Being under pressure from outside?
  • Now

    Which of the hard moments do we both consider closed, and which one is still quietly open?

What we got wrong about each other

  • Six months

    What did you assume about me at the start that turned out not to be true?

  • Now

    What have you come to like about me that you didn't notice at first?

  • Now

    What do you now know about me that you're still adjusting to?

    Not a complaint — an adjustment. There's a difference and we should both hear it as one. Say it like this“I didn't expect ___. I'm not upset by it, I'm still working out how to ___.”

What went unsaid

Note — rule for this block only

When one of us shares something we swallowed, the listener's only permitted responses for the first minute are: one clarifying question, and "thank you for telling me." No defending, no context, no "but you also…". We come back and respond properly after both of us have gone.

  • Six months

    Something I swallowed instead of saying.

    Say it like this“When ___ happened, I felt ___, and I didn't say it because ___.”
  • Six months

    A time I said "it's fine" and it wasn't.

  • Now

    Say it like this“What I'd want more of is ___, and it would look like ___.”
  • Now

    What made it feel safer to say nothing?

    This is the important one. Whatever the answer is, it's something we built together — not a flaw in whoever stayed quiet.

The pattern

  • Six months

    What's the argument we keep having in different clothes?

    Different topic each time, same feeling underneath. Name the feeling.
  • Now

    What reliably sets it off — the situation, not the person?

    Tiredness, distance, being around other people, plans changing at short notice, one of us being busy.
  • Now

    Who usually moves first to fix it, and does that feel fair to both of us?

    If the same person always repairs, that person is spending something. Say what it's costing them.
  • Ahead

    Has the pattern got better, worse, or stayed the same over six months — and where is it heading?

    Direction matters more than the current position. A slowly improving hard thing is very different from a stable one.
02 Money and how we'd liveFinancial philosophy · standards ▾

Money arguments are almost never about money. They're about safety, freedom, and fairness.

Why this matters

We're not sharing a household yet, so nothing has been tested. That's exactly why now is cheap and later is expensive: it costs nothing today to discover we picture different things, and it costs a great deal to discover it in year three with a joint account and a mortgage. Before we pick a system, we each say which of the three — safety, freedom, fairness — we're actually protecting.

What we've already seen

  • Six months

    How have we actually handled money together so far — who pays for what, and has it ever felt awkward?

  • Six months

    Has either of us made an assumption about the other's spending that we never checked?

  • Now

    What's the money situation you're most afraid of ending up in?

    Say it like this“What I'm protecting is ___. What I'm afraid of is ___.”

Three decisions, not one

Do this

"How we handle money" is three separate questions collapsed into one, which is why couples talk past each other. Separately, each of us writes an instinctive answer to all three — whatever comes first, not the diplomatic version:

  1. Earning — who brings income in, and what happens if that changes?
  2. Managing — who runs the day to day: bills, tracking, decisions under a set amount?
  3. Owning — whose savings are they, and who has a say on the big ones?

    Where did our instincts match, and where did they differ?

  • Ahead

    For each one: is that instinct something you actively want, or just what you grew up seeing?

  • Ahead

    Does either of us believe that whoever earns should be the one who decides?

    Say it plainly if the answer is partly yes. It's a common belief, and an unspoken one is far more corrosive than a stated one.

Four ways couples do it

None of these is the right one. Read them, then say which is closest and what we'd change.

Full pool

All income into one account. Both draw from it. No "my money."

Works whentrust is high and incomes will swing over a lifetime. Strains whenspending styles differ, or one person starts feeling watched.

Proportional share

Both put in the same percentage of income toward shared costs. What's left is personal.

Works whenincomes are unequal but both want the burden to feel equal. Strains whenthe lower earner still ends up with much less freedom in absolute terms.

Yours, mine, ours

A joint account for household costs and shared goals. Personal accounts stay personal, no explanations owed.

Works whenboth value independence and both earn. Strains whenone stops earning, or "personal" quietly grows to cover things that should be joint.

Operator and steward

One income covers running costs; the other is saved and invested rather than spent.

Works whenboth agree the saved half belongs to both of us, and the operating half isn't treated as the more important job. Strains whenthe one saving feels she has no claim on what she didn't spend, or the one operating feels his whole worth is the payslip.
  • Ahead

    Which is closest to what each of us pictured, and what would we change about it?

  • Ahead

    In whichever model we lean toward: whose name is on what, and who can see everything?

  • Ahead

    What's the amount above which we always ask each other first?

    Pick an actual number today. "Big purchases" is not a number.

The "normal" gap

Do this

Separately, each of us writes what normal looks like — with real numbers where we can:

a weekday dinner · a night out · a holiday · a phone · clothes in a month · the home we'd live in five years from now · how much goes to savings before anything else.

Note — before comparing

A gap in standards isn't a character flaw in either direction — not in the one who wants more, not in the one who wants less. What ruins couples is an unspoken gap that turns into quiet judgement on one side and quiet shame on the other. So we name the number, not the vibe.

  • Now

  • Ahead

    Who feels that gap more day to day, and what would it actually cost each of us to move toward the other?

  • Now

    Is there anything the other person spends on that you've quietly judged?

    Say it kindly, once, so it can be discussed rather than carried. Say it like this“When you spend on ___, I notice I feel ___. I'd rather ask about it than keep thinking it.”
  • Ahead

    What do our values or faith say about what we owe, what we give away, and what we keep — and do we read that the same way?

03 Time together, apartQuality time · online and in person ▾

We don't live together. Almost every hour we get is one somebody chose to make.

Why this matters

Couples who live together get time by accident — they're in the same kitchen. We don't. Every call, every trip, every protected evening exists because one of us built it. That makes this the area where drift is easiest and slowest to notice, and where a small habit repeated for a year quietly becomes the whole relationship. This part is about how much of each other we get and what it feels like. Part 04 is about what happens inside that time when it goes wrong — they're different questions.

Note — the trap in this part

It's easy for this to become one person defending their schedule while the other lists grievances. Both of us have work, both of us have people who need us, and both of us have been the distracted one. Every question here gets answered by both of us, even where it seems obvious that it's about one of us.

What our time actually looks like

Do this

Separately, each of us adds up last week, not an ideal week: hours we spent together in person · hours on call or video · hours messaging · and the longest stretch we went without real contact. Then compare.

  • Now

    Of those hours, which felt like really being together, and which felt like being on a call while doing something else?

    This is the whole distinction the part turns on. Quantity and quality are not the same measurement and we should stop treating them as one.

    What makes an hour together feel full for you? What makes one feel empty?

  • Now

    Wanting less contact isn't wanting less of each other. But if one of us reads it that way, we need to know that now.
  • Now

  • Now

  • Six months

When we're together but not really

  • Now

    Both answer. Neither answer is a confession; they're just different problems with different fixes.
  • Now

    Say the feeling and the story separately. The story is usually the part that hurts, and it's usually not what the other person meant. Say it like this“When ___ happens, I feel ___, and I start believing it means ___. What I need to know from you is ___.”

04 How we treat each otherConflict · repair · respect ▾

Almost nobody leaves over a disagreement. They leave over how the disagreement was handled, a thousand times.

Why this matters

Part 03 was about how much of each other we get. This one is about what happens inside that time — especially when it goes wrong. Everything else on this page is a topic we could in principle avoid. This is the one we can't: whatever we decide about money or family or where we live, we will be doing it with these two tempers, in these two languages, for the rest of it.

What each of us does when it hurts

  • Now

    When you're hurt, what's your first instinct — go quiet, get loud, get logical, leave, fix it immediately, make a joke?

  • Six months

    Your own worst moment, not theirs. Going first with this changes the whole block.
  • Now

  • Now

  • Now

    Does arguing over text or call change how either of us behaves?

    Worth naming: at this distance, most of our conflicts happen through a screen. That's a different medium with different failure modes — silence reads as punishment, tone disappears, and either of us can simply stop replying.

The floor

Do this

05 Boundaries with other peopleUnderstand first, agree second ▾

The first time, we went straight to rules. This time: reasons first, agreement second.

Why this matters

A rule you don't believe in is one you have to keep remembering instead of just living, and remembering fails eventually — not through dishonesty, just through being human. That's why the month-one version didn't hold. If we can find the shared reasoning underneath, we won't need to police anything. If we can't find it, that's the single most important thing this whole day could tell us, and we'd both rather know it now than at year three.

Note — read out loud before starting

Neither of us is on trial. We're both bringing a history, a set of instincts about friendship, and a comfort level we've never fully explained to each other.

Two things stay true for this whole part. One: nobody has to apologise for who their friends were before we met, or for anything that predates an agreement between us. Two: if one of us has been anxious about this, that anxiety is his or hers to say, not to enforce quietly. Silent watching costs a relationship more than any friendship ever has.

What each of us is actually protecting

  • Now

    What do your friendships give you that you'd genuinely miss?

    Answer honestly, or the agreement we write later will be about something neither of us understands.
  • Now

    What are you actually afraid of here? Say the fear, not the rule that comes from it.

    "I'm afraid of being humiliated." "I'm afraid of being controlled." "I'm afraid of slowly losing my friends." "I'm afraid of not being trusted no matter what I do." All of these are real, and none of them is the same problem. Say it like this“What I'm afraid of is ___. What I'd need in order to feel okay is ___.”
  • Now

    Is a boundary a fence protecting something, or a cage limiting someone? When has one felt like each to you?

  • Six months

    Is there anything we agreed in month one that you kept but never actually agreed with?

    The amnesty clause applies here, hard. Saying this out loud is the single most useful thing that can happen in this part.
  • Now

    What's the difference, for you, between a boundary you keep because you agreed to it and one you keep because you believe in it?

    The second kind survives without anyone checking. The first kind doesn't — and that's not a character flaw, it's just how borrowed rules work.

Preference or belief

Note — the honest question of this part

After we've each explained ourselves, some gap will probably remain. There are two very different kinds:

  • A preference gap — we want different amounts of the same thing. These get negotiated, and both of us can move without losing ourselves.
  • A belief gap — we actually think different things are right. These can't be negotiated away. They can only be shared, or consciously accepted with eyes open.

Each of us says which kind we think this is about ourselves, not about the other. "For me this is a belief." "For me this is a preference." Nobody diagnoses the other person.

  • Ahead

    If it's a belief gap: can each of us live with the other holding that belief, honestly?

    Not "can I get them to change." Can I live with it if they don't.
  • Ahead

    If it's a preference gap: what's the version we can both live inside — not the version one of us endures?

07 Growth, and what change does to usThe uncomfortable one ▾

We're both going to become someone slightly different. The question is whether each of us can stay when the other does.

Why this matters

Everything else on this page describes the two people we are today. This part is the only one that asks about the two people we'll be in ten years — and those are the people who actually have to stay married. Most couples who drift apart didn't disagree about anything on the earlier lists. One of them changed, and neither had ever said out loud what they'd need if that happened.

If one of us gets further ahead

Note — run it both ways

This is the question most couples skip because raising it feels disloyal. It isn't. Assume for ten minutes that one of us ends up with significantly more income, status or authority than the other — and answer both sets of questions below, each of us, whichever of us it turns out to be. Then swap and do it again with the roles reversed.

  • Ahead

    As the one further behind: what exactly are you afraid of? Say the specific version, not "I'd feel bad."

    Being spoken to differently. Not being consulted. Being introduced as an afterthought. Having your opinion carry less weight at home. Name the actual moment you're picturing. Say it like this“What I'm picturing is ___. What would make it bearable is ___.”
  • Ahead

    As the one further behind: what would make you feel small, and what would make you feel proud of them instead?

  • Ahead

    As the one further ahead: what do you fear — being resented, having to shrink it, having to explain it?

  • Now

    Have you ever downplayed a success to keep the peace with someone? What did that cost you?

  • Ahead

    Name the specific behaviour — not the money — that would tell each of us we're still respected in that scenario.

  • Ahead

    What's the early warning sign that this is going wrong, and what do we do about it then rather than three years later?

The limits of change

  • Ahead

    What kind of change in me would you struggle to accept?

  • Now

    Is there anything you're hoping I'll grow out of?

    Say it. Marrying someone on the assumption they'll become a different person is the most common way this goes wrong, and it's usually done sincerely.
  • Six months

    Is there anything about me you'd want changed that I've shown no sign of changing in six months?

08 The non-negotiablesWritten apart, read together ▾

The things where, if it went the other way, we could not build a life here.

Why this matters

Everything before this was negotiable. This part is the floor underneath it. Doing it near the end matters — after a day of honesty we know much more about what we actually need than we did this morning, and the lists we write now will be truer than the ones we'd have written before breakfast.

Do this

Separately, in different rooms, no discussion. Each of us writes a maximum of five, as short plain sentences.

The test: if this went the other way, would I be unable to build a life here? If the honest answer is "I'd hate it but I'd stay," it's a preference and it belongs in an earlier part. If you have more than five, they're preferences.

Note — if something lands in conflict

Don't solve it today, and don't panic about it today. A genuine conflict between two non-negotiables is the single most important thing this whole day could have surfaced — finding it is the process succeeding, not the relationship failing. Write it down, sit with it separately for a week, and give it its own conversation.

09 Remaining doubtsThe part people skip ▾

A doubt said out loud shrinks. A doubt kept becomes the story we tell ourselves later about why it didn't work.

Why this matters

This is the part the first conversation didn't reach, and it's why the first conversation didn't hold. Everything else today can be answered sincerely and still leave the real thing unsaid. If we only do one part properly, it should be this one.

Note — how to say one, and how to hear one

Saying it. Use the shape below. It keeps the doubt about a situation and a need, instead of about the other person's character.

Hearing it. Three jobs, in order: don't defend yourself for the first sixty seconds; ask one question; say thank you. Respond properly afterwards — but not in that first minute, because the first minute is what teaches us whether it's safe to do this again.

Each of us, in turn

  • Now

    The doubt I've had for a while and haven't said.

    Say it like this“When ___ happens, I worry that ___, and what I need to know from you is ___.”
  • Six months

    The thing I'm afraid is a pattern rather than a moment.

    Say what would tell you it's only a moment. Giving a worry a way to be disproven is the difference between a worry and a verdict.
  • Ahead

    Something I'm afraid you'd have to change about yourself for this to work — and whether I even want you to.

  • Now

    Something I'm doing that I know isn't fair to you.

    This one is about yourself, on purpose. The part is only survivable if both of us are exposed in it.
  • Now

    What I need from you that I've been embarrassed to ask for.

  • Ahead

    If we don't work out, what would the reason most likely be?

    Answer honestly. It's the most useful question here, and hearing it isn't an announcement that either of us is leaving.

After

  • Now

    What did each of us hear that we want to sit with rather than answer today?

  • Now

    Is there anything either of us said today we want to be sure the other doesn't misread? Say it again, in different words.

  • Now

    Say thank you, out loud and properly, for the hardest thing the other person said today.

Note — one last thing to say to each other

Whatever came up today, the other person chose to tell you rather than keep it. That was the harder option, and it was a form of commitment. Say so.

Today wasn't for proving we're right for each other, and it wasn't for catching anyone out. It was for replacing what we assume about each other with what we've actually said out loud.

If something came up that doesn't resolve, that isn't the day failing. That's the day doing its job. Clarity first. The decision comes after — and it comes easier.

We're pausing

No reason needed. Nothing is lost by stopping here.

  1. Stop the topic where it is. Don't finish the sentence.
  2. Ten minutes minimum, apart. Drink some water.
  3. Whoever called the pause says when they're ready — not the other one.
  4. Come back and say what you were feeling, not what they were doing.
  5. If it's still too much, park the topic for another day and move to the next part. That's allowed and it isn't a failure.

The line either of us can use at any time:

“That's a verdict, not a question. Can we reset?”

And what we promised at the start: honesty gets thanked, not punished. Nothing said today gets used later.
